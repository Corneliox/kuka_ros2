\begin{abstract}
Seamless and contactless human-robot interaction (HRI) is essential for flexible manufacturing cells and sterile assistive workstations. However, integrating multimodal perception (speech and computer vision) with proprietary industrial robot controllers often suffers from non-deterministic communication latencies, tedious manual calibration routines, and labor-intensive validation protocols. In this paper, we present an end-to-end, deterministic multimodal framework and an automated benchmarking pipeline for color-coded material handling using Robot Operating System 2 (ROS~2) and a 6-DOF KUKA KR6 R900-2 industrial manipulator. The architecture incorporates an offline, privacy-preserving Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) engine (Vosk) augmented with phonetic alias compensation, a perspective-corrected 2D planar homography mapping based on a 12-marker ArUco constellation, and MoveIt~2 utilizing the deterministic Pilz Industrial Motion Planner. Execution commands and state telemetry are exchanged with the KUKA KRC4 controller over TCP/IP via the Ethernet KRL Interface (EKI). Furthermore, we propose a fully automated benchmarking architecture that eliminates manual intervention during multi-trial experiments by programmatically synchronizing perception queries, trajectory dispatch, kinematic state logging, and error estimation. Initial physical baseline validation on the hardware testbed demonstrates an average decision latency of \SI{1.26}{\second}, a mean Cartesian position error of \SI{3.18}{\milli\meter} ($\pm\SI{0.33}{\milli\meter}$), a mean joint tracking error of $1.55^\circ$ ($\pm 0.11^\circ$), and a \SI{100.0}{\percent} pick-and-place success rate across preliminary trials. This study offers an accessible, low-compute solution for reliable, hands-free collaborative manipulation on commercial industrial hardware.

\keywords{Human-Robot Interaction \and ROS 2 \and Industrial Manipulator \and KUKA EKI \and Multimodal Perception \and Automated Benchmarking \and Planar Homography \and MoveIt 2.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

In modern collaborative manufacturing, flexible assembly workstations, and sterile medical environments, contactless human-robot interaction (HRI) enables operators to direct robotic manipulators without diverting their hands from delicate manual tasks~\cite{quirubot2016,schafer2023smart}. While lightweight collaborative robots (cobots) have proliferated in recent years, heavy-duty industrial manipulators—such as the KUKA KR6 Agilus series—remain irreplaceable in industrial production due to their superior rigidity, high structural acceleration, and sub-millimeter trajectory repeatability ($\pm\SI{0.03}{\milli\meter}$).

Despite these mechanical advantages, integrating commercial industrial robot controllers (e.g., KUKA KRC4) with high-level multimodal artificial intelligence (AI) pipelines presents significant architectural hurdles:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Vendor Controller Isolation}: Industrial controllers traditionally run proprietary real-time operating systems executing vendor-specific code (e.g., KUKA Robot Language, KRL) designed for static cyclic operations rather than dynamic sensor-guided adaptation.
    \item \textbf{Interfacing Latency and Jitter}: While specialized interfaces such as the KUKA Fast Robot Interface (FRI) provide hard real-time ($<\SI{1}{\milli\second}$) cycle control, they are largely restricted to specialized research robots (such as the LBR iiwa) and require costly proprietary options~\cite{lbr_stack2024,kuka_eki_eval2022}. Standard industrial robots must rely on Ethernet KRL Interface (EKI), which introduces soft real-time communication delays (\SI{12}{\milli\second} to \SI{50}{\milli\second}) that must be compensated for at the trajectory generation layer.
    \item \textbf{Computational Burden and Cloud Dependency}: Modern vision-language-action (VLA) models and cloud-based Large Language Models (LLMs) introduce non-deterministic execution latencies (\SI{>2}{\second}) and require continuous internet connectivity, which violates strict latency, reliability, and data privacy requirements in industrial and medical facilities.
    \item \textbf{Labor-Intensive Experimental Workflows}: Traditional validation of robotic manipulation pipelines relies heavily on manual operator intervention—such as manually capturing images, computing offline coordinates, triggering individual motion commands, pausing the robot, and manually recording spatial deviations. This manual overhead induces severe operator fatigue, restricts sample sizes, and introduces human measurement inconsistencies.
\end{enumerate}

To address these challenges, this paper presents a lightweight, deterministic multimodal framework and an automated benchmarking suite for color-coded material handling and handover using ROS~2 and a KUKA KR6 R900-2 industrial manipulator. Standardized color coding—a universally adopted organizational paradigm in industrial Kanban bin sorting and medical instrument cassettes—serves as an efficient and deterministic semantic layer. 

The primary contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Modular ROS~2 Multimodal Stack}: A decoupled software architecture connecting an on-device offline ASR engine (Vosk), a perspective-corrected ArUco planar homography vision node, and MoveIt~2 trajectory planning to a commercial KUKA KRC4 controller over standard EKI TCP/IP sockets without specialized hardware cards.
    \item \textbf{Deterministic Industrial Motion Execution}: Implementation of the Pilz Industrial Motion Planner (LIN and PTP primitives) within MoveIt~2, ensuring predictable, collision-free Cartesian approaches and retractions with tool center point (TCP) offset compensation ($Z_{\text{offset}} = \SI{76}{\milli\meter}$).
    \item \textbf{Automated Experimental Benchmarking Architecture}: An end-to-end orchestrator that eliminates manual testing bottlenecks by autonomously triggering execution cycles, capturing perception queries, measuring real-time joint and Cartesian tracking errors, and logging multi-trial datasets.
    \item \textbf{Physical Hardware Validation}: Rigorous baseline verification on the physical KUKA KR6 R900-2 testbed evaluating latency breakdown, joint tracking errors, Cartesian accuracy, and establishing the protocol for full 50-run multi-condition expansion.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Industrial Robot Interfacing within ROS}
Bridging external computing nodes with proprietary robot controllers has been a cornerstone of open-source robotics research. Sanfilippo et al.~\cite{jopenshowvar2015} introduced JOpenShowVar, an open-source cross-platform interface enabling read/write access to KUKA KRC variables over TCP/IP. Kunz et al.~\cite{lbr_stack2024} developed the LBR-Stack, providing standard \texttt{ros2\_control} hardware drivers for KUKA LBR manipulators via Fast Robot Interface (FRI). While FRI provides direct joint-torque and high-frequency position control, it is unavailable on standard industrial Agilus and Cybertech series robots. For these widely deployed manipulators, the Ethernet KRL Interface (EKI) provides a manufacturer-supported XML-over-TCP/IP communication link. Vargas et al.~\cite{kuka_eki_eval2022} evaluated industrial communication protocols and noted that while EKI exhibits higher jitter than RSI, combining EKI with client-side trajectory parameterization in ROS enables highly reliable sensor-guided manipulation.

\subsection{Multimodal Human-Robot Collaboration}
Voice-guided robotic manipulation has been explored across diverse collaborative domains. Mendoza-Larios et al.~\cite{quirubot2016} proposed Quirubot, a robotic scrub nurse utilizing speech recognition and 2D image processing to fetch surgical tools. Schäfer et al.~\cite{schafer2023smart} demonstrated a multimodal assistant using acoustic intent parsing and deep learning for surgical tool handover. Radhakrishnan et al.~\cite{voice_ros2_2024} implemented a voice-controlled pick-and-place pipeline in ROS~2, noting that environmental acoustic noise often impairs cloud-based ASR transcription. In contrast to cloud-reliant frameworks, our proposed approach integrates an offline Kaldi-based Vosk engine~\cite{vosk2020} with phonetic alias sets, guaranteeing deterministic execution latency and absolute data localization.

\subsection{Planar Visual Localization and Fiducial Markers}
Fiducial markers, notably ArUco~\cite{aruco2014}, are extensively utilized for robot camera calibration and pose estimation. In planar industrial environments, homography transformations provide direct projective mappings from 2D camera pixel coordinates to the robot's base coordinate frame. While single-marker calibration is vulnerable to lens distortion and perspective tilt at the workspace boundaries, distributing a multi-marker constellation across the workspace enables robust global projective compensation via Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), eliminating the need for iterative visual servoing.

\section{System Architecture and Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The overall system architecture is depicted in Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}. The framework comprises five tightly integrated subsystems: (1) Hardware and Controller Network Interface, (2) Offline Speech-to-Intent Pipeline, (3) Perspective-Corrected Vision Engine, (4) MoveIt~2 Industrial Motion Planning Layer, and (5) Automated Benchmark Orchestrator.

\subsection{Hardware Configuration and Network Layer}
The physical hardware setup consists of a 6-DOF KUKA KR6 R900-2 sixx Agilus industrial manipulator (maximum payload: \SI{6}{\kilogram}, horizontal reach: \SI{901}{\milli\meter}, position repeatability: $\pm\SI{0.03}{\milli\meter}$) driven by a KUKA KRC4 compact controller running KUKA System Software (KSS) 8.3. A custom vacuum gripper is affixed to the mechanical tool flange (\texttt{tool0}). The suction cup contact plane defines the active Tool Center Point (TCP) with a calibrated vertical offset of:
\begin{equation}
    Z_{\text{offset}} = L_{\text{base}} + L_{\text{bellows}} = \SI{60}{\milli\meter} + \SI{16}{\milli\meter} = \SI{0.076}{\meter}
\end{equation}

Communication between the ROS~2 workstation and the KRC4 controller is established over a dedicated point-to-point Gigabit Ethernet link (Workstation IP: \texttt{192.168.1.100}, KUKA KRC4 IP: \texttt{192.168.1.147}, Port: \texttt{54600}). The KRC4 controller executes a native KRL daemon (\texttt{ros\_eki.src}) configured with an EKI XML communication channel. State telemetry ($\mathbf{q}_{\text{cmd}}, \mathbf{q}_{\text{act}} \in \mathbb{R}^6$) is broadcasted at \SI{20}{\hertz}, while Cartesian trajectory waypoints are ingested cyclically. Vacuum gripper actuation is dispatched via a dedicated Boolean topic \texttt{/gripper\_cmd} bridged through the EKI motion socket to prevent channel concurrency locks.

\subsection{Offline Speech-to-Intent Pipeline}
To eliminate cloud dependency and ensure sub-second response times, speech recognition is executed entirely on the local host CPU using the Vosk ASR engine~\cite{vosk2020} loaded with the lightweight \texttt{vosk-model-small-en-us} acoustic model. Audio is captured via a unidirectional microphone sampled at $f_s = \SI{16}{\kilo\hertz}$ with an internal buffer size of 8000 samples.

Because small-footprint acoustic models are susceptible to phonetic substitutions under background acoustic noise, we define an invariant phonetic alias mapping matrix $\mathcal{M}_{\text{alias}}$. Target color intents are resolved using the following classification rule:
\begin{equation}
    c^* = \arg\max_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \mathbb{I}\left( w_{\text{detected}} \in \mathcal{S}_c \right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C} = \{\text{red}, \text{yellow}, \text{blue}, \text{green}\}$ denotes the set of recognizable component classes, and $\mathcal{S}_c$ represents the phonetic alias dictionary:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{S}_{\text{red}}    &= \{\text{red}, \text{right}, \text{read}, \text{rad}\}, \\
    \mathcal{S}_{\text{yellow}} &= \{\text{yellow}, \text{yeah}, \text{yell}, \text{hello}\}, \\
    \mathcal{S}_{\text{blue}}   &= \{\text{blue}, \text{do}, \text{woah}, \text{boo}\}, \\
    \mathcal{S}_{\text{green}}  &= \{\text{green}, \text{great}, \text{grain}, \text{greed}\}
\end{align}
Upon validation of an intent $c^*$, the node formats a JSON string and publishes it to the ROS~2 topic \texttt{/voice\_command}.

To achieve high-accuracy spatial localization without tedious hand-eye calibration, a constellation of 12 ArUco markers ($ID \in [0, 11]$) is rigidly fixed across the table surface. The ground-truth Cartesian coordinates $[X_i, Y_i]^T$ relative to the KUKA base frame origin $[0,0,0]^T$ are given in Table~\ref{tab:aruco_coords}.

During system calibration, the detected pixel centroids $\mathbf{u}_i = [u_i, v_i, 1]^T$ and their corresponding world points $\mathbf{x}_i = [X_i, Y_i, 1]^T$ are utilized to estimate the planar homography matrix $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ via Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) with RANSAC outlier rejection:
\begin{equation}
    s \begin{bmatrix} X_w \\ Y_w \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{H} \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13} \\ h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23} \\ h_{31} & h_{32} & h_{33} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
where $s$ is an arbitrary projective scale factor.

For online target localization, captured camera frames are converted to the HSV (Hue-Saturation-Value) color space. Binary masks $\mathcal{B}(u,v)$ are generated via color thresholding:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{B}(u,v) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } H_{\text{min}} \le H(u,v) \le H_{\text{max}} \land S(u,v) \ge S_{\text{min}} \land V(u,v) \ge V_{\text{min}} \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Following morphological opening and closing filters (using a $5\times 5$ elliptical kernel), the object's pixel centroid $(\bar{u}, \bar{v})$ is calculated from spatial image moments:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{u} = \frac{M_{10}}{M_{00}} = \frac{\sum_u \sum_v u \cdot \mathcal{B}(u,v)}{\sum_u \sum_v \mathcal{B}(u,v)}, \quad \bar{v} = \frac{M_{01}}{M_{00}} = \frac{\sum_u \sum_v v \cdot \mathcal{B}(u,v)}{\sum_u \sum_v \mathcal{B}(u,v)}
\end{equation}
The real-world target pick coordinate $\mathbf{x}_{\text{target}} = [X_w, Y_w, Z_{\text{pick}}]^T$ is then calculated via $\mathbf{H}^{-1}$ projection.

\subsection{Trajectory Planning and Deterministic Motion Layer}
Motion planning is governed by MoveIt~2 using the \texttt{pilz\_industrial\_motion\_planner}. To guarantee smooth, deterministic industrial motions, trajectory generation relies on standard Pilz motion primitives:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PTP (Point-to-Point)}: Executes fast non-linear joint-space interpolation for gross transfer motions between the resting park pose and the approach waypoint ($Z_{\text{approach}} = Z_{\text{pick}} + \SI{120}{\milli\meter}$).
    \item \textbf{LIN (Linear Cartesian)}: Executes straight-line vertical descent to the contact plane ($Z_{\text{pick}}$) and straight-line vertical retraction, preventing tool-to-workspace collisions.
\end{itemize}
Orientation constraints enforce a strict downward end-effector pose throughout the pick-and-place sequence:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{q}_{\text{ori}} = \left[ q_x = 0.0, \, q_y = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \, q_z = 0.0, \, q_w = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} \right]^T
\end{equation}
The planning scene is dynamically populated with collision objects via ROS~2 \texttt{ApplyPlanningScene} service requests to safeguard the robot against table collisions.

\section{Automated Benchmarking and Measurement Workflow}
\label{sec:benchmark_workflow}

\subsection{Architecture of the Automated Benchmarking Pipeline}
In contrast to prior experimental setups that required manual script execution and visual inspection between individual trials, we propose an automated benchmarking node (\texttt{auto\_benchmark\_runner.py}) paired with a passive telemetry logger (\texttt{benchmark\_logger.py}). The complete automated execution sequence is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:benchmark_workflow}.

\begin{algorithm}[tb]
\caption{Automated Benchmark Execution and Telemetry Logging}
\label{alg:benchmark_workflow}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Test Category $\mathcal{T}$, Target Color $c$, Total Iterations $N$
\STATE Initialize ROS~2 node and subscribe to \texttt{/gripper\_cmd}, \texttt{/task\_status}, \texttt{/joint\_states}
\FOR{$k = 1$ \TO $N$}
    \STATE Query vision service \texttt{/detect\_object}($c$) $\to$ Obtain estimated pose $[X_v, Y_v]$
    \STATE Broadcast start event: \texttt{/benchmark\_run\_start} $\leftarrow$ JSON($\mathcal{T}, k, c, X_v, Y_v$)
    \STATE Trigger task execution: \texttt{/voice\_command} $\leftarrow c$
    \STATE Record start timestamp $t_{\text{start}} \leftarrow \text{now}()$
    \WHILE{Task active \AND $(t - t_{\text{start}}) < T_{\text{timeout}}$}
        \STATE Compute joint tracking error $e_\theta(t) = \|\mathbf{q}_{\text{cmd}}(t) - \mathbf{q}_{\text{act}}(t)\|_1$
        \IF{\texttt{/gripper\_cmd} $== 1$ \AND $\neg \text{contact\_flag}$}
            \STATE Capture kinematic TCP position $\mathbf{x}_{\text{contact}} \leftarrow \text{FK}(\mathbf{q}_{\text{act}})$
            \STATE Compute spatial residual: $e_{\text{pos}} = \|\mathbf{x}_{\text{contact}} - [X_v, Y_v]^T\|_2$
            \STATE $\text{contact\_flag} \leftarrow \text{True}$
        \ENDIF
    \ENDWHILE
    \STATE Evaluate success flags: $S_{\text{task}}, S_{\text{pick}}, S_{\text{place}}$
    \STATE Broadcast end event: \texttt{/benchmark\_run\_end} $\leftarrow$ JSON($S_{\text{task}}, e_{\text{pos}}, \bar{e}_\theta, T_{\text{comp}}$)
    \STATE Append serialized telemetry to \texttt{benchmark\_results.csv}
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Experimental Benchmark Categories}
To rigorously characterize the system's performance, an experimental protocol is designed across four distinct operational regimes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline Benchmark (20 runs)}: Nominal pick-and-place trials across all four color classes under standard ambient laboratory lighting (\SI{450}{\lux}).
    \item \textbf{Physical Generalization (10 runs)}: Evaluation of pick-and-place accuracy when target components are placed at arbitrary workspace boundary positions and varying planar orientations.
    \item \textbf{Environmental Robustness (10 runs)}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Illumination Stress}: Validation under bright glare (\SI{900}{\lux}), dim lighting (\SI{80}{\lux}), and dynamic moving shadows cast across the workspace.
        \item \textit{Visual Occlusion}: Introduction of synthetic physical occlusions masking \SI{20}{\percent}, \SI{40}{\percent}, and \SI{60}{\percent} of the component's visible surface area.
        \item \textit{Clutter Interference}: Placing distractor items (extraneous cables, metallic tools, non-target objects) adjacent to the target component.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spatial Repeatability (10 runs)}: Successive pick-and-place cycles targeting a fixed drop zone to measure positional standard deviation ($\sigma_x, \sigma_y$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Quantitative Evaluation Metrics}
The benchmarking architecture automatically logs the following metrics for each trial:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Decision Latency ($T_{\text{dec}}$)}: Time elapsed between final acoustic utterance detection and the initial commanded joint velocity:
    \begin{equation}
        T_{\text{dec}} = t_{\text{motion\_start}} - t_{\text{voice\_end}}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Total Completion Time ($T_{\text{comp}}$)}: Total duration from voice command trigger to return to the final resting park pose.
    \item \textbf{Mean Joint Tracking Error ($\bar{e}_\theta$)}: Mean absolute tracking error across all 6 manipulator joints:
    \begin{equation}
        \bar{e}_\theta = \frac{1}{6 M} \sum_{j=1}^6 \sum_{k=1}^M \left| \theta_{j,\text{cmd}}(t_k) - \theta_{j,\text{act}}(t_k) \right|
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Planar Position Error ($e_{\text{pos}}$)}: Euclidean error between the visual centroid prediction and physical contact coordinates:
    \begin{equation}
        e_{\text{pos}} = \sqrt{(X_w - X_{\text{robot}})^2 + (Y_w - Y_{\text{robot}})^2}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Task Success Rate (\%)}: Percentage of runs successfully grasped, transported, and placed without vacuum loss or collision.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper demonstrated an end-to-end, deterministic multimodal manipulation framework and an automated benchmarking pipeline for color-coded material handling using ROS~2 and an industrial KUKA KR6 R900-2 manipulator. By integrating offline Vosk speech recognition, a 12-marker ArUco planar homography transformation, and MoveIt~2 Pilz industrial motion planning over KUKA Ethernet KRL (EKI), the system achieves hands-free manipulation with an average decision latency of \SI{1.26}{\second} and sub-\SI{3.5}{\milli\meter} positioning accuracy on physical hardware. The automated benchmarking architecture successfully streamlines multi-trial experimental workflows, logging real-time joint errors and Cartesian deviations without human intervention.

Future work will expand the perception layer to 6-DOF RGB-D point cloud registration for unconstrained non-planar objects and investigate on-device Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for open-vocabulary semantic task planning.

The empirical metrics presented in Table~\ref{tab:latency_breakdown} and Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_summary} of this manuscript reflect the initial physical validation stage (Runs 1 to 5) conducted on the physical KUKA KR6 R900-2 Agilus industrial manipulator at Asia University. These initial trials successfully verified the core architectural integration: (1) low-latency local speech recognition via Vosk, (2) reliable planar homography coordinate mapping from the 12-marker ArUco constellation, (3) deterministic linear and point-to-point motion execution via MoveIt~2 Pilz planner, and (4) bidirectional telemetry exchange with the KUKA KRC4 controller over the Ethernet KRL Interface (EKI) on port 54600.

\subsection*{Newly Formed Automated Protocol for Full-Scale Data Collection}
To establish statistical rigor across diverse operational conditions, a fully automated benchmarking pipeline (\texttt{auto\_benchmark\_runner.py} / \texttt{run\_master\_pipeline.py}) has been developed. This pipeline eliminates manual image capture, terminal command dispatch, and manual coordinate recording. The complete 50-run experimental matrix is actively being expanded following the structured protocol defined in Table~\ref{tab:protocol_roadmap}.
